What you're seeing

  • Size: about 60 × 60 cm and 30 cm tall. An object for the floor or a sideboard, not a table.
  • Body: glass-fiber reinforced concrete (about 20–25 kg), whose mass absorbs the verdict's vibration, holding a Mac Studio against the right wall, turned so it exhausts straight out the right vent through a short duct.
  • Brass vents on opposite sides: perforated brass on the left (air in) and the right (air out), so air crosses straight through past the Mac. More air enters through the gap at the base.
  • Gap: 4 cm on four brass posts with firm rubber isolators. The back-right post is hollow and carries the one-way meter wire.
  • Sand tray: a thin brass plate on a vibration driver at its center, edges free, driven at resonance so it barely has to move. Sand: round glass microbeads, about 100–200 micrometers, one size, dry.
  • Dust cover: thin glass, lifts off. It keeps out dust, humidity and cats.
  • Raking light: a low LED strip along one inside edge, so every ridge of sand throws a shadow.
  • Front edge of the tray: the key and four telltales, at hand height.
  • The tone is the plate: no speaker. The brass rings at the resonance that draws the figure, so the sound and the sand can't disagree; uncertainty makes it sound grainy, and it rings down like a bell. Felt under the dust cover stops buzzing; the controller caps how hard it's driven.
  • Gap light: the Float's state seam, around the top of the body under the tray. Steady gold when on, slow breathing when asleep, and when off a small heartbeat blink by the back-right post, so off never looks the same as broken. It can be turned off.
  • Brass ages: unlacquered brass darkens to brown over a few years and can take a faint green in crevices over decades. The key, handled daily, stays brighter. Lacquered brass stays bright until the lacquer wears.
  • Honesty: measured from the model's internals, one-way wire, radio-free controller, a mapping unique to the house and never seen by the agent.

The one principle: honesty

This object exists to show, physically, how an AI agent's work actually went: not what the agent says about itself, but what was measured inside it. Every design choice serves that. Other principles are welcome but not required.

  • Measured, not reported. The reading comes from the model's internals (how sure it was, how hard it worked), never from its own account.
  • Unseen by whoever is measured. The agent can't see, hear or reach the display. One wire, one direction, a controller with no radio, nothing in common on any network.
  • Hard to fake. Which figure and pitch mean what is generated on the controller at setup, unique to the house, and never seen by the agent. Readouts are never used to train a model.
  • One event, no disagreement. The sound and the figure are the same vibration of the same brass plate.

How it works

  1. The agent works on a task, running a local model on the Mac Studio in the concrete body. The sand table stays still and silent, still showing the last verdict.
  2. A meter program, separate from the agent, reads the model's internals as it works: how spread its word choices were (uncertainty), how much it did (effort), and which tools it used.
  3. When the task ends, the meter sends a few numbers out the one-way wire, up the hollow post, to the controller in the tray.
  4. The controller looks up the house's private mapping, then drives the brass plate at one of its resonances for a few seconds. The more uncertain the agent was, the more noise it adds to the drive.
  5. The plate rings; the sand jumps and settles along the still lines. A sure agent leaves a crisp figure and a clean tone; a guessing agent leaves a smeared figure and a grainy tone. Then it rings down like a bell and goes quiet.

Build steps

  1. Run the desk test (below). It decides the plate size, the pitches, the drive limit and whether the blur reads.
  2. Write the meter and controller firmware on the bench: meter on the Mac, one-way wire, controller driving the desk-test plate. Generate the house mapping.
  3. Make the tray: walnut frame with the sand gutter, the brass plate on its driver, raking light, key and telltales on the front edge, felt for the glass.
  4. Cast the concrete body with vent openings, the back hatch pocket and stainless inserts. Fit the brass vents and duct after it cures and is sealed.
  5. Assemble: Mac against the right wall, controller and power strip inside, wire up the hollow post, posts and isolators, tray on top, sand and glass.
  6. Tune: confirm each verdict's figure and loudness in the room.
  7. Heat test: a long agent run with the body closed; check the Mac's own temperature readings.

Risks and open questions

  • Loudness. Plates can be shrill. Use the low resonances only and cap the drive. The biggest livability question.
  • Plate size. Large plates have many close resonances, which makes clean figures harder. 35–40 cm may beat 50+.
  • Sand loss. Free edges shed sand each verdict; the gutter catches it and the cover lifts for a top-up.
  • The blur. Adding noise should leave the sand less settled; how well that reads is untested.
  • The meter software. Possible with llama.cpp or MLX, but it's custom work.
  • Heat. Should be fine with vents on opposite sides; measure it.
  • The renders. The figure on this page is an idealized drawing, not a simulation.

What to do

  1. Bolt a plate to the driver at its center, over the catch tray. Sprinkle a thin, even layer of microbeads.
  2. Sweep the tone slowly from about 100 to 1,500 Hz. Note every frequency that makes a clean figure. Photograph each.
  3. At each good frequency, measure loudness at 1 m with the drive just high enough to form the figure.
  4. Add noise to the tone at three levels (low, medium, high). Photograph how the figure blurs. This tests the core idea.
  5. Weigh the sand, run 20 verdicts, weigh what's left on the plate. That's the sand-loss rate.
  6. Repeat on the other plate size, and with silica sand.

What to bring back

  • The list of clean frequencies and their loudness, per plate size.
  • Photos of each figure, crisp and at the three noise levels.
  • Sand lost per 20 verdicts.
  • Which plate size and sand gave the cleanest, quietest result.
