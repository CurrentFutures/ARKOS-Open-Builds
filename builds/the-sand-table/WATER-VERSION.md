# The Sand Table: glass water version (spec draft)

A variant of the Sand Table that uses a shallow glass tray of water instead of sand on brass. Vibration forms standing-wave patterns in the water, and a light below projects them, magnified, onto the ceiling. Everything about honesty stays the same: the meter, the one-way wire, the radio-free controller.

## How it works

1. A shallow glass tray holds a few millimetres of water, sealed under a glass lid. It sits on the same vibration exciter the brass plate would use.
2. When a task ends, the controller shakes the tray up and down at a chosen frequency for a few seconds.
3. Above a certain strength, the water surface forms a regular standing-wave pattern (Faraday waves): stripes, squares or hexagons, depending on frequency and depth.
4. A small, point-like light in the gap below shines up through the water. The ripples act as moving lenses and project the pattern, much larger, onto the ceiling.
5. The tray then rings down and the water goes calm. Unlike sand, water doesn't hold the pattern, so the projected light is the live display, and the calm surface is the resting state.

## What changes from the brass version

| Part | Brass version | Water version |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | 1 mm brass plate | Shallow glass tray, roughly 30 to 40 cm square, sides about 1 to 2 cm high |
| Medium | Glass microbeads | Distilled water, a few millimetres deep, with a little biocide to stop algae |
| Lid | Lift-off glass dust cover | Sealed glass lid to stop evaporation and dust |
| Light | Raking strip along one edge | One small, bright, point-like LED in the gap, pointing up; a diffuse light would wash the pattern out |
| Display | The figure on the plate | The pattern projected on the ceiling, plus the live surface seen from above |
| Sand gutter | Needed | Not needed |
| Sound | The plate rings like a bell | Mostly a low hum while driven; quieter, less musical |

Everything else stays: the concrete body, the meter, the one-way wire, the radio-free controller, the key and telltales, and the heartbeat light.

## How the water shows a verdict

Water has a useful property: below a threshold strength the surface stays flat, just above it a clean, regular pattern appears, and well above it the pattern breaks into chaotic ripples.

- **Settled:** drive just above the threshold. A clean, steady lattice of light on the ceiling.
- **Unsettled:** drive harder, or add noise. The lattice breaks into restless, shifting light.
- **The household's figure:** chosen at setup from the patterns the tray can make, as with the brass version. Clarity means confidence in every home.

**The one thing water loses: memory.** Sand holds the last figure until the next task. Water goes calm within seconds. Options to keep the last verdict readable:

1. Replay the verdict for a few seconds whenever someone presses the key or walks up.
2. Hold a very gentle, near-silent pattern until the next task, if the hum is livable.
3. Let the two mechanical counters (tasks and unsettled tasks) carry the record, with the water as the live moment.

Option 3, with option 1 as a bonus, keeps the room quiet and the record honest.

## Desk test for the water version

Run it the same afternoon as the brass desk test, on the same exciter and amplifier.

**Extra parts:** a shallow glass baking dish or a picture-frame glass with a sealed silicone rim, distilled water, a small bright LED torch, a white ceiling or a sheet of white card held above, and a darkened room.

1. Mount the dish on the exciter. Add water about 2 mm deep, then try 5 mm.
2. Shine the LED straight up through the water from below, onto the ceiling or card.
3. Sweep slowly from about 10 to 150 Hz. Note where clean patterns appear and how strong the drive needs to be.
4. At each good frequency, push the drive harder to find where the pattern breaks into chaos. This is the settled-to-unsettled range.
5. Measure loudness at one metre, and note how long the water takes to go calm.
6. Photograph and film the ceiling.

**Bring back:** the frequencies and depths that make clean patterns, how loud they are, the threshold-to-chaos range, the calm-down time, and the best footage.

## Risks, and a microgravity version

**Risks**

- **Water near electronics.** The Mac and controller sit below the tray. Use a sealed tray, a drip channel that drains away from the electronics, and a moisture sensor in the body that shuts the drive off.
- **Algae and haze.** Water plus light grows algae. Use distilled water, a biocide, and a sealed lid; plan an easy refill.
- **Ceiling height and colour.** The projection needs a pale ceiling at a reasonable height. Test in the real room.
- **The hum.** Lower frequencies hum rather than ring. The desk test decides whether it's livable.
- **Level.** A tilted tray gives uneven patterns. Add levelling feet.

**A microgravity version**

Without gravity, water has no free surface, but surface tension can hold one. A thin water film pinned at sharp edges inside a sealed glass cell would stay in place, and at small scales its ripples are driven by surface tension rather than gravity, so patterns can still form, finer and at higher frequencies. Spacecraft fuel tanks already use surface tension to place liquids. This version would need testing on a parabolic flight or in a drop tower before anyone could claim it works.
